Motivation

Habits pay off in years but cost effort today, and today usually wins.

I wanted to play guitar ten minutes a day. I downloaded a streak app, kept it up for nine days, missed one, watched the counter drop to zero, and stopped opening it. My classmates told me the same story with running, Spanish and drawing.

Who has the problem. Anyone trying to build a small daily habit: practise an instrument, read, learn a language, run. In my test group of six, five use no habit app at all; the sixth uses Notion.

How they handle it today. Willpower, a to-do list or a streak tracker. Trackers record what you did but give nothing back on a low day, and a missed day resets the number to zero, which is exactly when people quit. One tester summed up the plain tracker: "felt nothing to do."

What it costs. In a well-known study of 96 people, a new daily habit took a median of 66 days to become automatic (range 18–254; Lally et al., 2010). Most people give up long before that, losing the money they spent on courses, gyms and apps, and the version of themselves the habit would have built.

Why care. The pay-off of a habit is years away; the effort is today, so today wins. Research on future self-continuity finds that people who feel connected to their future self make more patient choices: seeing an age-progressed image of themselves led people to put more money into retirement savings (Hershfield et al., 2011). Future Feed applies that idea to everyday habits.

What is new here

Two ideas no habit app combines: meet the you who kept going, and build a town to get there.

Idea 1 · the why

Meet the you who kept going

  • Every check-in brings a postcard from your future self, dated the year you'd reach that moment at your pace.
  • It is a continuing story with the people you named, not a random quote.
  • It is honest: your real check-in rate decides how far the future you can go, and an AI checker rewrites anything that overclaims.
  • Slip for a few days and future you writes worried, never shaming.
Idea 2 · the habit loop

Build a town, one check-in at a time

  • Each habit is a building in your felt town.
  • Each check-in moves into a room that keeps that day's postcard.
  • A full floor of rooms adds a storey outside (1, 2, 4, 7, 16, 30 check-ins).
  • Miss three days and a storm rolls in, with a 3-day comeback quest to clear it.

Why the two ideas work together. Idea 1 gives a reason (a vivid, believable future you); Idea 2 gives a reward you can see grow every day. Postcards are what fill the rooms, so the "why" and the daily loop are the same object.

Model: gpt-6-luna for every agent. It is the only model on the course key and it is cheap ($0.10 / $0.50 per million input / output tokens). Because each agent has one narrow job and is checked by the next, a small model is enough: it judged 3 of 3 test captions correctly (Safety), and testers rated motivation 6.3 / 7 (Impact).

One real run, step by step (October 26, a fresh account)

  1. Sign-up. Aisling names a friend (Jess) and picks Play guitar, 10 min and Read, 20 min.
  2. Game engine. First check-in → level 1, "Floor 1 built". Allowed: "can play a few open chords cleanly and strum a simple song at home." Not yet: "plays a full easy song for friends." At her pace this moment is dated February 2027.
  3. Story agent writes: "A clean G, C, and D—and then I strummed my first simple song all the way through in the living room. Jess got the first kitchen-side concert 🎸", with comments from Jess and Mom, a letter, and a stamp titled "OPEN CHORDS".
  4. Reality-check agent catches an overclaim: "'Jess got the first kitchen-side concert' implies playing for a friend, beyond the allowed at-home milestone", and removes that sentence.
  5. Photo tool + safety agent choose and approve "Gypsy Jazz Acoustic Guitar" (CC BY 2.0).
  6. Result. The postcard arrives, the room fills, and the Music hall appears in town. Logged: 1,899 input tokens (1,064 cached), 1,060 output tokens, ≈ $0.0007.

How I kept it cheap

Small model, narrow jobsgpt-6-luna everywhere, 100× cheaper than gpt-6-astra; each agent does one thing and is checked by the next.
CachingFixed instructions first and user data last, so 1,064 of 1,254 story input tokens are cached. Photo verdicts are cached by URL; the library was vetted once (19 of 230 removed).
Hard limitsStrict JSON and an output cap on every call; one check-in per habit per day; a daily token cap (300,000). Pre-written postcards are reused, never written twice.
A cost cut that hurt, and the evidenceThe story agent's output cap was 1,500 tokens. After I added the stamp design its replies grew, and in my A/B dry run a slump postcard was cut off mid-JSON (server log: JSONDecodeError: Unterminated string); the home page failed to load. Fix: cap 3,000, one retry with more room, and a failed slump post can never block the page. Real outputs are 900–1,100 tokens, so the higher ceiling costs nothing on normal runs. A second trade-off: custom habits began with a small photo pool, and a tester noticed "same postcard picture, it's fake". Now unused photos come first, and a habit fetches more when it runs out.

Limits and next steps

A strong first signal from six people; the next step is two weeks of real check-ins.

What doesn't work yet
  • Small, friendly sample: 4 complete pairs and 6 people in total, one session each; two people's A answers weren't saved. Intentions are self-reported, and novelty helps B. A strong, consistent early signal, not proof.
  • The slump is the weakest moment: only 1 of 6 found the storm motivating, 2 found it discouraging, 2 didn't notice it.
  • The style isn't for everyone: one tester found it "too cute for a man."
  • Local and single-user: no accounts, reminders or phone app; photos are stock photos, not your own life; a live postcard takes 8–16 s when it can't be pre-written.
With another month
  • A 2-week field test that measures real check-in rates, not intentions.
  • A hosted, multi-user version with daily reminders.
  • A gentler, more visible slump rescue: future you asks for help, and the comeback rebuilds the storm-damaged floor.
  • A calmer theme for people who find felt too cute.
  • Visit friends' towns and cheer on their buildings.
